A full gut in San Marcos, and the longest of these projects at ten weeks. The extra time went into the joinery: cabinetry and a hood built for this room rather than specified from a catalogue, and stone templated to suit.

Cabinetry

Custom white oak throughout, including a run of tall cabinetry that carries to the ceiling. Building to the room rather than to a modular grid is what removes the filler strips and the bulkhead above the uppers — the details that quietly mark a kitchen as assembled rather than made.

White oak was the right species here. It takes a light finish without going orange the way some oaks do, and the grain has enough character to carry a large expanse without needing a second material to break it up.

The hood

Custom, built for the space. The hood is the one element in most kitchens with nothing beside it to hide behind — it sits alone above the range at eye level, which is exactly why an off-the-shelf unit so often reads as chosen rather than designed. Built to the room, it sits in proportion with the cabinetry either side and gives the wall a focal point.

Quartzite counters

Custom quartzite, templated to the cabinetry as installed. Quartzite is quarried stone, so every slab is unique — which means it is worth seeing the actual slab rather than a sample chip, because what arrives is what you looked at.

It also handles heat in a way engineered surfaces do not. A pan straight off the burner will mark quartz; quartzite takes it. On a kitchen where cooking is the point, that matters more than the specification sheet suggests.

Flooring

New hardwood throughout, running continuous from the kitchen into the rooms around it so the floor reads as one surface rather than stopping at a threshold.

The result

A kitchen where the expensive decisions are the ones you cannot see from a photograph — cabinetry built to the millimetre of the room, a hood made rather than bought, and stone chosen slab by slab. Ten weeks from demolition to final walkthrough.
