Living Through a Remodel: How to Prepare Your Home and Your Routine

Practical preparation for staying in your home during a renovation — dust, access, pets, temporary setups and communication.

Most people stay in the house while the work happens. It goes considerably better when the disruption has been planned for rather than absorbed as it arrives.

Set up the temporary version first

If the kitchen is coming out, you will need somewhere to make coffee, heat food, and wash up. Establish that before demolition rather than the morning after.

A folding table in the dining room with the microwave, kettle, toaster and a drying rack covers most of daily life. Access to a sink matters — a laundry room or bathroom sink usually becomes the washing-up point. Paper plates for the duration are not a failure of standards.

The same logic applies to a bathroom project. Confirm which bathroom stays in service and make sure the route to it does not cross the work area.

Pack the room out completely

Empty the space entirely, including the cabinets you are convinced will not be touched. Dust travels, and work areas shift as a project develops.

As you pack, separate what you use weekly from what you use twice a year. The weekly items go somewhere accessible; the rest can be boxed and stored. Most people discover in this process that a fair amount does not need to come back at all.

Dust is the main event

Demolition and sanding produce fine dust that finds gaps you did not know existed.

  • Plastic sheeting at doorways, with zippered access where people need to pass through
  • Covering or sealing return air vents in the work area, so the HVAC system does not distribute dust through the house
  • Rolling up and removing rugs nearby rather than covering them in place
  • Protection on the floors along the route the crew walks, not only in the room itself
  • Replacing air filters after the dusty phases

Good containment makes an enormous difference, and it is reasonable to ask how it will be handled before work begins.

Pets and children

Doors get propped open, tools sit within reach, and unfamiliar people move in and out all day. For an anxious animal, a room on the far side of the house with the door closed is kinder than trying to manage it in the moment.

It is worth telling the crew which animals are in the house and which doors must stay shut. Most of the genuine near-misses come from a door left open by someone who did not know.

Access and the working day

Sort out early how the crew gets in, where they park, where materials are staged, and what hours suit you. Waiting for someone to arrive and let them in wastes the day for both sides.

If you work from home, mention it. Noise levels vary hugely across a project, and knowing the loud phases in advance lets you plan calls around them.

Expect the sequence to shift

Renovations move in dependent stages: what is behind the walls has to be right before the walls close, and surfaces cannot go on until what is underneath is ready. When something is uncovered — and in older homes, something usually is — the schedule adjusts.

This is normal rather than a sign of trouble. The useful posture is to expect adjustment and ask to be told about it as it happens.

Agree how you will communicate

Decide up front who you speak to about questions and changes, and how. A single point of contact and a regular check-in prevents the situation where a decision gets made on site because nobody could reach you.

When you do change something mid-project, put it in writing, even briefly. Not out of suspicion — simply because a written note is what everyone can refer back to weeks later.

The part people underestimate: the last ten percent — final fittings, adjustments, touch-ups — often takes longer than expected relative to how finished the room already looks. Knowing that in advance makes it much easier to live with.
